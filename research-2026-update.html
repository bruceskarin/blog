Institutional Corruption: The 2026 Update

Running for office costs a fortune, so politicians spend their time courting big donors instead of voters. The donors get what they want; everyone else gets ignored. That rigs the economy toward the rich, which makes regular people give up on politics — which hands even more power to the donors. It’s a vicious circle, and it works the same from city hall to Congress.

Ten years ago I built a computer simulation of that circle. This is the update: new data through 2024, a corrected model, and an honest answer about what it would take to break the cycle.

What I got wrong in 2015

The original study’s headline stat was that max-out elite donors gave 233 times what small donors (under $200) gave. That number was wrong — the data source I relied on undercounted small-dollar contributions roughly 11-to-1. Recomputed from raw FEC filings:

  • 2014: the true ratio was about 19-to-1, not 233-to-1.
  • 2020: small donors outgave elite max-out donors to House campaigns ($234M vs $216M).
  • Small-dollar giving grew 25x from 2014 to 2024. Big money (elites + PACs) grew 1.3x and still outspends small donors about 3-to-1 in absolute dollars — but its dominance collapsed.

Online fundraising transformed who funds campaigns. The model had no mechanism for it, so I built one — and recalibrated everything on the corrected data. Trust in the model now actually tracks reality: it falls to about 20%, matching the data, and income inequality reaches 6.5 against 6.7 observed.

But the deeper problem got worse

  • Trust kept falling — from 24% (2014) to 20% (2024). More small donors didn’t fix it.
  • Lobbying hit records — $4.4 billion in 2024, up from $3.2 billion in 2015.
  • The money chase intensified — House campaigns topped $2.1 billion in 2024. Candidates spend more time fundraising than legislating.

Here’s the uncomfortable finding: trust isn’t driven by campaign money directly. It’s driven by who policy favors. Campaign finance reform can cut elite contributions in half — but the $4.4B lobbying industry keeps the policy bias in place. The money is a symptom; the policy outcomes are the disease.

What would it take? The model’s answer

I tested four paths starting in 2027, measuring public trust in government in 2060:

ScenarioTrust in 2060What it means
Do nothing14.9%The bleak baseline. Trust keeps sliding.
Finance reforms (vouchers + 9:1 small-dollar match + cost controls)43.7%+29 points. This reverses my 2015 verdict — with correct data, matching works.
Reduce elite leverage over policymaking31.7%+17 points. This is changing how policy gets made, not who funds it.
Everything combined55.2%+40 points. A credible recovery path.

No single reform does it alone. But the combination — public money big enough to displace elite money, plus cost controls, plus less leverage for donors over the legislative process — breaks the cycle. Getting back to 75% trust is aspirational; getting to 55% is achievable.

The honest bottom line

Small donors proved ordinary citizens will fund campaigns at scale. That means reforms amplifying their voice have a real constituency. But democratizing fundraising without democratizing access doesn’t fix the core problem — a $25 online donation doesn’t get you a meeting; the max-out donor and the lobbyist still do.

The story shifted from “elites buy elections” (depressing, disempowering) to “the system forces everyone to chase money” (structural, fixable). That’s more hopeful — and more honest.
