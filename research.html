Institutional Corruption: 2026 Update

In 2015, I presented research at the International Conference of the System Dynamics Society titled U.S. Campaign Finance and Institutional Corruption in Public Policy. That work mapped how campaign funding, lobbying, policy bias, income inequality, and voter trust form a self-reinforcing cycle that erodes representative democracy.

Ten years later, I've updated the data, the model, and the story. Some things changed dramatically:

  • Small donors rose up. In 2014, max-out elite donors gave 19x what small donors (under $200) gave to House campaigns. By 2020, small donors actually outgave elites. Online fundraising transformed who funds campaigns.
  • But trust kept falling. Public trust in government dropped from 24% (2014) to 20% (2024). More small donors didn't fix the deeper problem.
  • Lobbying hit records. Federal lobbying spending reached $4.4 billion in 2024, up from $3.2 billion in 2015.
  • The money chase got worse. Total House campaign spending topped $2.1 billion in 2024. Candidates spend more time fundraising than legislating.

The updated model tests reforms against this new reality: democracy vouchers, small-dollar matching, media cost controls, and transparency requirements. The findings are both sobering and hopeful — the system is more responsive to small donors than we thought, but the fundamental dynamics of money, access, and trust remain.
